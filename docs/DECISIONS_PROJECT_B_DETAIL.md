# Project B: full decision log

The detailed version of the Project B section of `DECISIONS.md` (which is a 1-page summary to keep the file within 3 pages). Every major choice, in the order it was made, with the alternatives and why they were rejected. Numbers come from the pipeline outputs in `outputs/project_b/`.

| # | Decision | Alternatives considered | Why rejected |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Keep all assumptions (columns, holdout length, budget, experiment details) in `config/project_b.yaml` | Hard-coded constants | Same reason as A1: assumptions change live in the interview |
| B2 | Hold out 13 consecutive weeks, default = the last 13 (2023-10-23 to 2024-01-15); `--holdout-start` moves the window and snaps to the next week in the data | Random held-out weeks | Random weeks leak information through adstock (an ad's effect carries into neighbouring weeks), and the real use is forecasting forward |
| B3 | Model **revenue**, not conversions | Conversions as the outcome | ROI and the budget are in money; conversions are an index, not people (DATA_ISSUES B7) |
| B4 | Check the experiment against our own data before using it | Take the vendor's 0.8 as given | The task warns the test quality is unknown; the check shows spend never dropped (DATA_ISSUES B1) |
| B5 | Draw the causal graph first and test its assumptions on training weeks only (`project_b/dag.py`) | Put every column in and let the model sort it out | Some columns could be mediators (results of ads); adding a mediator hides part of the ad effect. Testing first tells us which are safe |
| B6 | Treat **competitor sales** and **sentiment** as confounders → control variables | Leave them out (simpler); treat them as mediators (results of our ads) and leave them out | Both rise in the same weeks as our spend (corr 0.50 / 0.58 after removing region and week averages). Leaving them out flips the spend effect from +0.19 to −0.41 (ads look harmful). Mediator reading rejected: adding them *raises* the spend effect, the opposite of what a mediator that lifts sales would do |
| B7 | Include **Promo** as a non-media treatment | Leave it out | Moves with spend (0.31) and lifts revenue (0.13). Left out, its lift is credited to ads (spend effect 0.19 → 0.54) |
| B8 | Include **Organic** as organic media | Leave it out; treat it as a mediator of paid media | The task asks for it. It moves with paid spend (0.46) but adding it changes the paid effect by only −0.01, so the mediator question barely matters |
| B9 | Use **impressions** as the media input and **spend** only to compute ROI | Spend as the media input; both as separate drivers | Price per 1,000 impressions is fixed, so they are copies (DATA_ISSUES B3); both = double-counting. Impressions is Meridian's standard input; with a fixed price the ROI is identical either way |
| B10 | Region intercepts (hierarchical) + population scaling + time effects | National pooling only; no time effects | Region size drives spend and revenue (0.997 / 0.97). Spend repeats yearly (autocorr 0.50 at 52 weeks) even though revenue does not (0.04): time effects stop the model crediting ads for calendar patterns |
| B11 | Known limit: the "last week" test for mediators is weak | — | Spend barely carries over week to week (autocorr 0.12), so "last week's spend does not predict this week's controls" (all ≈ 0) is only weak support. The sign test (B6) carries the argument |
| B12 | Use Meridian's own hold-out (`HoldoutSpec` with one date range for all regions), built from step 0's `is_holdout` flag | Cut the hold-out weeks out of the data; random hold-out cells | Meridian hides only revenue in those weeks and keeps ad spend, so ads shown just before the window still carry over into it (adstock). Cutting the weeks out would break that. One flag set in step 0 keeps every step on the same split |
| B13 | Build the national data by adding regions up: volumes summed, controls and promo population-weighted, revenue per conversion = total revenue ÷ total conversions | Simple average of controls; national average of revenue per conversion | Controls and promo are intensities, not volumes; a big region's value should count for more people. Ratio-of-totals keeps national revenue exactly equal to the sum of regions (checked) |
| B14 | Score three naive forecasts on the hold-out before modelling (last 13 weeks' average, same week last year, training average) | Report the model's accuracy on its own | A number like "5% error" means nothing without a baseline. Naive forecasts miss the national weekly total by 4.7–5.4% and track none of the week-to-week movement (R² below 0); the model must beat that in step 6 |
| B15 | Google Meridian for the Bayesian MMM | PyMC-Marketing, NumPyro / Stan written by hand | The data is Meridian's own sample format; Meridian has geo hierarchy, adstock + Hill saturation, ROI priors, experiment calibration and a budget optimiser built in and tested. Hand-written code would add risk with no modelling gain. Trade-off: less control over internals, and the library's quirks (e.g. it needs `kpi_type='non_revenue'`, not the `'non-revenue'` shown in its own docstring) |
| B16 | Time effects with one knot every 13 weeks (12 knots), none inside the hold-out | Meridian's geo default (one knot per week); a single knot (no time effect) | One knot per week gives the hidden weeks time effects with no revenue to learn from, so the forecast would be driven by the prior. No time effect leaves calendar confounding in (spend repeats yearly, step 1). Quarterly knots follow seasonal swings in spend while keeping ~520 region-weeks per knot. Knots next to the hold-out carry the last level flat into it |
| B17 | ROI priors (LogNormal, median 1.22, 90% 0.28–5.4), **same for every channel** | Meridian's coefficient priors (`beta`); different priors per channel; the vendor's 0.8 for Channel3 already here | ROI is the scale a CFO can reason about, so the prior can be defended in business terms. Same prior for all: we have no channel-specific evidence before step 5, and putting the experiment in now would leave nothing to compare against |
| B18 | Adstock decay Uniform(0,1); half-saturation TruncatedNormal(0.8, 0.8, 0.1–10); Hill slope fixed at 1 | Informative decay priors per channel; free slope (S-curves) | No evidence on carry-over length, so let the data decide. With weekly impressions an S-shape is rarely identifiable and makes the half-saturation point wobble; slope = 1 is the standard choice for impression data |
| B19 | Organic share of revenue Beta(1, 99) (mean 1%); promo share TruncatedNormal(0, 0.1) | Same wide priors as paid media | Step 1 found organic barely relates to revenue once paid media is in; promo can lift or cannibalise, so it is centred on zero and the data picks the sign |
| B20 | 4 chains × (1,000 adapt + 500 burn-in + 500 kept); gate on R-hat ≤ 1.1 | 400 adapt + 300 burn-in (first run); fewer chains | The first run converged for every media parameter but not for the baseline: time-effect levels (R-hat 1.15) and region intercepts (1.13) trade off against each other (raise one, lower the other, same fit), and one chain sat elsewhere on that ridge. Longer adaptation fixed it (all R-hat ≤ 1.04) at modest cost (~7.5 → ~11 min on a 2-CPU machine). 4 chains are the minimum for a reliable R-hat. A `--quick` mode exists only to test the pipeline |
| B21 | Posterior-predictive check **with noise** (model mean + simulated error), scored on training and hold-out weeks | Compare only the model's mean to actuals | The mean alone always looks too confident. With noise, a well-calibrated model should hold ~90% of actuals inside its 90% band, and that can be checked |
| B22 | **Revised the baseline priors after the prior-predictive check**: time level Normal(0, 2), region baselines Normal(0, 1), controls Normal(0, 2), between-region spread of control/promo effects HalfNormal(0.5) | Keep Meridian's defaults (Normal(0, 5), HalfNormal(5)); tighten everything to Normal(0, 1) | The defaults let revenue go **negative** before seeing data in 24% of national weeks and 35% of region-weeks. Normal(0, 1) everywhere was too tight: the prior band of average revenue (8.7–13.7M) no longer contained the actual 8.45M. The chosen set cuts negatives to 4% / 12% and keeps actual revenue inside the band every week. Region baselines are tied to the data's own spread (±1 sd per person). Media priors (B17–B19) were not touched: they looked sensible (prior paid-media share of revenue 13–59%). **Sensitivity:** ROIs under the default vs revised baseline priors differ by at most 0.10 (Channel0 1.09 → 1.01, Channel3 1.18 → 1.08, Channel4 1.35 → 1.25; Channel1–2 within 0.04), and fit accuracy is identical, so the conclusions don't hinge on this choice. Convergence improved (lowest ESS 59 → 114) |
| B23 | Step 1's bias check uses a standard OLS call (`statsmodels`, region and week dummies) | Hand-written least squares with manual standard errors | Same numbers exactly; one standard line is easier to read, check and defend |
| B24 | National model = **exactly** the geo model's spec (same priors, knots, hold-out, sampler) on summed data | Re-tune priors for the national model | Any difference must come from aggregation, not from different assumptions; otherwise the comparison says nothing |
| B25 | **Trust the geo model**, not the national one | Trust the national model (simpler, narrower ROI ranges); average the two | National ROIs are 0.30–0.67 lower (Channel3 0.40 vs 1.08). Four measured reasons: (1) 40× fewer data points (143 weeks vs 5,720 region-weeks); (2) 64–80% of each channel's spend variation exists only between regions and is added away; (3) nationally, total spend moves in lock-step with the competitor confounder (0.75 vs 0.50 within regions), so credit can't be split; (4) the quarterly trend absorbs up to 40% of national spend movement (Channel3) but only 8% of revenue's. The national model also learned **nothing** about adstock (0–2% narrowing vs prior; geo up to 36%) and predicts worse on the hidden weeks (R² −0.17 vs +0.08). Its *narrower* ROI ranges come from the model's structure, not more information, a red flag rather than a strength. Caveat: the geo model assumes regional spend differences aren't targeted at regions expected to sell more |
| B26 | Flag: **neither model learns the saturation point** (posterior range ≈ prior range for every channel) | — | Marginal ROI and the budget split (steps 6–7) therefore lean on the saturation prior. Step 7 must show how the allocation changes under different saturation assumptions before anyone acts on it |
| B27 | Feed the experiment in as a **prior on Channel3's ROI during the 8 test weeks** (Meridian's ROI calibration window) | Extra likelihood term; a prior on Channel3's ROI over all 3 years | The vendor measured ROI in specific weeks, so the prior should describe those weeks, not 3 years with different spend and season. A prior is Meridian's built-in route; an extra likelihood term would mean modifying the library's model code for no clear gain. Limitation: Meridian calibrates by time, not region, so the prior covers all regions in those weeks, not just Geo0–Geo7. The model's own estimates for test regions vs all regions are close (1.23 vs 1.19), so this matters little |
| B28 | **Weight the vendor's result at 25%** (a "power prior": its uncertainty is doubled, then combined with our usual prior). Fit full trust (100%) alongside for comparison | Full trust; ignore it (0%); a mixture prior "25% chance the test is valid" | Evidence against: our records show spend never dropped (2 of 64 zero weeks, +18% spend), so if the data is right the test didn't happen as described. Evidence for: the data might record planned rather than delivered spend. Plus: small regions only, unknown design, a vendor with a stake. 25% is a judgement, so we show it doesn't matter: Channel3 ROI 1.08 / 1.05 / 0.97 at 0 / 25 / 100% trust. The mixture prior was tried first and rejected: Meridian's sampler can't handle that distribution type |
| B29 | Use the **calibrated model (25% trust)** for steps 6–7 | Uncalibrated model | It uses all the evidence we have, at a weight we can defend, converged (R-hat ≤ 1.03), and fits the hidden weeks equally well (national MAPE 3.8%, R² 0.08). With results this close, the choice is about honesty, not outcome |
| B30 | Marginal ROI = extra revenue from **1% more spend in the last 13 weeks** ÷ that extra spend | Marginal ROI over all 3 years; derivative of the response curve at average spend | The budget decision is about next quarter, and the last 13 weeks are the closest picture of "current spend" (they are also the ±30% reference). A 1% step is small enough to be the slope at today's level |
| B31 | Report ROI on **revenue**, and state that profit break-even is higher | Profit ROI | The data has no margins. With a gross margin m, a channel only pays for itself if ROI > 1/m (e.g. 40% margin → 2.5). Marginal ROIs are below 1 even on revenue (0.44–0.71), so the case for not adding spend holds whatever the margin |
| B32 | Report **chance above break-even** next to each range | Ranges only | "P(marginal ROI > 1) = 4%" is easier for a CFO to act on than a 90% interval |
| B33 | Model "next quarter" as a repeat of the last 13 weeks, each channel's week-by-week and regional pattern kept and scaled | Re-plan flighting week by week; use a whole year's average pattern | We have no plan for next quarter's timing; the last 13 weeks are the most recent picture of how each channel is bought. This is Meridian's standard assumption, and it means we optimise how much each channel gets, not when |
| B34 | Apply the ±30% rule to **last-13-week spend**, converting it into Meridian's terms for each budget, and refuse budgets the rule can't meet | Meridian's own ±30% (measured from a proportional split of the new budget) | Meridian's default would let a channel fall to 61% of current spend at 18M, breaking the brief. With the rule as written, the total can only be 14.5M–27.0M, so **12M is impossible**. We show two honest options instead (same % cut for all; best split with cuts allowed to −70%) and let the CFO choose which rule to break. A hard check stops the run if any "within rule" plan breaks it |
| B35 | Compute every plan's extra revenue from all 2,000 draws, and compare each plan with "same % cut for every channel" draw by draw | Use the optimiser's own interval per channel | The optimiser maximises the average; the CFO also needs (a) a range on the total and (b) how likely the recommended split actually beats the simple alternative. Comparing on the same draw cancels the shared uncertainty about overall media strength |
| B36 | Saturation stress test without re-fitting: split the draws into thirds by their half-saturation point and re-optimise on each | Re-fit with three different saturation priors (3 × 20 min) | The data doesn't pin down saturation (step 4), so the posterior on it ≈ the prior; conditioning on low / middle / high values approximates what different priors would give, in minutes instead of an hour |
| B37 | Lead the recommendation with the **budget level**, and present the split as a modest, robust tilt | Lead with the optimised split | The split beats a same-% cut by only ~0.11M (−0.49M to +0.64M; 66% chance it's better), while each step in budget moves revenue by far more: 18M → 20.8M adds 1.46M revenue for 2.77M spend (0.53 per rupee; 1% chance it pays back). The tilt (cut Channel0/1 ~29%, raise Channel2 ~20%, trim Channel3/4) holds under fast, middle and slow saturation, so it's safe to act on, just not where the money is |
| B38 | Rank channels by the **value of perfect information**: how much better each channel's budget could be set if we knew its true return (extra revenue minus spend, 13 weeks, within ±30%) | Rank by width of the ROI range; by spend; pick the vendor's channel | Width alone ignores whether the uncertainty could change a decision; spend alone ignores how well we already know the channel. Result: **Channel4** (~88k per quarter), then Channel2 (58k), Channel3 (42k). Channel3 is the biggest channel but the best measured, and 95% of draws already agree on trimming it |
| B39 | Size tests with **fake experiments on our own history** (2,000 random region sets × start weeks where nothing was switched off), analysed by **time-based regression** on a 52-week pre-period | Textbook power formula with assumed variance; simple before/after ratio; analysing with the MMM | Using our own data captures real regional noise. Time-based regression was ~40% less noisy than the ratio estimator; analysing with the MMM was about as noisy and would make the test depend on the model it's meant to check. Regions are assigned size-balanced (one per population block) so treated and control match in size, unlike the vendor's test (B3) |
| B40 | Accept that **no test pays for itself in budget terms within a year**, and say so | Present a test as if it were clearly worth it | The value of a realistic test (sample information) is ≤ 12k per quarter for every channel, because the data already agrees on what to do (80–95% of draws say cut), so even a perfect answer rarely changes the decision. Region revenue is noisy relative to any single channel (each is 1–7% of revenue), so tests are imprecise. The case for testing is to check the model independently, not to fine-tune next quarter |
| B41 | Recommend testing **Channel3**: switch it off in 20 size-balanced regions for 12 weeks, measure 2 more weeks, with a hard check that spend really is zero | Test Channel4 (costliest uncertainty); a smaller Channel3 test; no test | It's the cheapest design reaching 80% power to detect a true ROI of 1 (83%; ROI to ±0.63; range shrinks 29%). Channel4's best test reaches only 41% power, ±1.16, and costs ~437k against ~46k of information. Channel3's expected net cost is ~181k because its ROI is near 1 (revenue lost ≈ spend saved), and it saves money if the true ROI is below 1. It also re-runs the vendor test properly. 20 regions × 8 weeks (68% power, ~121k) is the cheaper fallback |
| B42 | Build the memo **by code from the saved tables** (`project_b/memo.py`, reportlab), with no typed-in numbers | Write the memo by hand in a word processor | The brief requires one command to reproduce every number in the memo. Generated from the CSVs, the memo can't drift from the analysis, and it rebuilds itself when the hold-out window or a prior changes. Trade-off: plainer layout; reportlab is one small, pure-Python dependency |
| B43 | Memo order: decision first (cut, tilt, don't act on the vendor), then evidence, then limits | Method first | A CFO reads the first box and maybe the tables. The method lives in DECISIONS.md; the memo states only what changes a decision and what the model can't tell them |
